## Summary

DRIFT aims to shed light on the configuration, as well as the change or persistence, of citizens’ territorial preferences in democratic regimes. By territorial preferences, we refer to individuals’ inclinations regarding a specific vertical distribution of power within a country. Thus, although preferences related to supranational integration have also been characterized from a territorial perspective within the framework of multilevel governance theory (Hooghe and Marks 2008), this project focuses primarily on the former, while treating the latter as a secondary but complementary dimension.

To date, the literature has largely focused on analyzing territorial preferences in countries where the center–periphery divide is particularly pronounced and where centrifugal forces—whether autonomist or even secessionist—are present. Moreover, most empirical efforts in this area rely on public opinion surveys which, although valuable, tend to capture these preferences only in a static manner and within specific institutional and social contexts. The widespread diffusion of diverse decentralization reforms, and the resulting transformation in the territorial organization of decision-making power across most democratic systems, make it necessary to pay closer attention to how these individual preferences are formed.

To address this gap, the project pursues three main objectives. First, it seeks to develop a comprehensive theory of the key economic, political, and cultural factors that shape the configuration of these preferences broadly defined—from more centripetal orientations to more centrifugal ones, including individuals satisfied with the institutional status quo. Second, it proposes to design a comparative public opinion study that combines survey methods with experimental approaches, with the aim of effectively isolating the identity-based, ideological, and material components underlying these preferences. Third, the project emphasizes the importance of social and institutional legacies by constructing a novel dataset compiling cantonal-level results from the April 1969 French regionalization referendum—previously unused data that will allow for a more behavioral and historically grounded approximation of these preferences in a context marked by an apparently centripetal societal dynamic.

Overall, the project aims to deepen our understanding of the nature and key features of territorial preferences in democratic settings, with the broader goal of better explaining the institutional and territorial evolution of countries through the use of multiple levels of analysis and diverse data sources.
