# WORKING PAPERS

### Decentralization by Design, Democracy by Default: De Gaulle's 1969 Gambit (with Filippo Liviano D'Arcangelo) <br/><a href="./rp/drift.html#featured-paper"><button type="button button1">DRIFT</button></a>

<details style="margin:-0.4em 0 1.4em;"><summary style="cursor:pointer;font-size:14px;color:var(--muted);">Abstract</summary><p style="margin:.6em 0 0;font-size:15px;line-height:1.6;">How much democratic backsliding will citizens accept to secure a policy they want? We argue the answer depends on whether the trade-off itself is partisan or policy-based. In April 1969, Charles de Gaulle bundled decentralization with a Senate reform that stripped it of legislative power, tying his presidency to the outcome and putting this trade-off to French voters directly. We isolate the policy-based mechanism, focusing on France’s center-periphery cleavage, with digitized referendum returns (1958–1969), a 1967–1969 panel survey, and de Gaulle’s campaign speeches. Regionalist departments moved further toward the reform than the rest of the country; sympathy for sub-national self-determination predicted the vote net of partisanship; and voters and de Gaulle himself split along the same line: the reform’s substance at the periphery, his rule elsewhere. Policy-based trade-offs may be harder to resist than partisan ones: citizens who accept a weaker check for a good they want are not excusing their side, but see the exchange as legitimate.</p></details>

### Territorial prejudice reduction through reciprocity (with Sandra León) <br/><a href="" target="_blank"><button type="button button1">Manuscript</button></a> <a href=""><button type="button button1">Appendix</button></a> <a href="./rp/fedcrisis.html#working-papers-fedcrisis"><button type="button button1">FEDCRISIS</button></a> {#wp-territorial-prejudice}

<details style="margin:-0.4em 0 1.4em;"><summary style="cursor:pointer;font-size:14px;color:var(--muted);">Abstract</summary><p style="margin:.6em 0 0;font-size:15px;line-height:1.6;">Territorial prejudice — negative affect toward residents of other regions — represents an understudied source of intergroup tension in (polarized) federal and decentralized democracies. We examine whether indirect reciprocity can reduce such prejudice under favorable conditions, exploiting the large-scale deployment of cross-regional firefighters following the catastrophic October 2024 Valencia floods. A pre-registered survey experiment among 1,500 Valencian residents randomly assigned participants to conditions varying whether their neutral or most-disliked outgroup region had provided assistance, measuring willingness to reciprocate in a hypothetical future disaster. Territorial prejudice significantly reduced cooperative intentions toward disliked regions by 3.5%, rising to 5% among respondents with the most negative outgroup affect, even amid overwhelming prosocial behavior and salient interregional solidarity. Indirect reciprocity produced no countervailing effect. These findings suggest that territorial prejudice constitutes a robust attitudinal disposition that persists even under conditions most favorable to its attenuation, with important implications for intergovernmental cooperation in polarized, decentralized democracies.</p></details>
